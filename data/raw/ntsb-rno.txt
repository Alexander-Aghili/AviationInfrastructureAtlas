C.     SUMMARY

        On August 30, 2016, about 1801 Pacific daylight time, a Beechcraft A-36 Bonanza,
N985CA, was destroyed when it impacted the ground in a recreational vehicle (RV) park about
one-half mile prior to the threshold of runway 16L at Reno/Tahoe International Airport (RNO),
Sparks, Nevada. The airplane was owned by Venture Aviation Services of Reno Nevada, and was
on leaseback to, and operated by, Flying Start Aero, a flight school based at RNO. Both persons
on board received fatal injuries; there were no ground injuries. The flight was conducted under the
provisions of Title 14 Code of Federal Regulations Part 91. Visual meteorological conditions
prevailed.

D.     DETAILS OF THE INVESTIGATION

        On September 21, 2016, the air traffic control group convened remotely and conducted an
interview via telephone with the RNO local controller working on the day of the accident. (See
attachment 1.)

E.     FACTUAL INFORMATION

1.0    History of Flight
         Bonanza N958CA entered the RNO airspace under visual flight rules (VFR) from the south
for landing at RNO. The NCT approach controller had issued the pilot a vector of 350 with an
altitude restriction to maintain 8,500 feet above mean sea level (msl). As N958CA approached
RNO from about 6 miles southeast, the NCT approach controller advised the pilot of N958CA
there would be a “slight delay for runway 25, ah you’re number four now for the airport, ah traffic
on a fifteen mile straight in is a Boeing seven fifty seven for one-six right.” The pilot acknowledged

and stated that he could accept runway 16L. The NCT approach controller instructed the pilot of
N958CA to enter the downwind and to contact RNO tower; it was not specified which runway.

       At 1754:33, the pilot of N958CA contacted the RNO local controller, “Reno tower good
afternoon, Bonanza niner eight five Charlie Alpha restricted heading three-five-zero, eight
thousand five hundred.”

        At 1754:42, the RNO local controller answered a landline call from the NCT approach
controller who informed him that the pilot of N958CA could accept runway 16L; would be the last
aircraft for runway 16L; and, would be entering from the downwind.

      At 1754:50, the RNO local controller instructed the pilot of N958CA to continue for
runway 25; the pilot acknowledged. However, the pilot continued for runway 16L without the
RNO controller correcting the pilot.

       At 1755:08, an unknown pilot believed to be the pilot of N958CA transmitted “and ah
tower, how about the restrictions?” There was no response by the RNO local controller.

      At 1756:20, the pilot of N958CA reported that he was on a wide downwind for runway
16L. The RNO local controller instructed the pilot to continue for runway 16L; the pilot
acknowledged.

       At 1757:12, the pilot of N958CA advised that he was turning a wide left base for runway
16L. The RNO local controller acknowledged and informed the pilot “traffic, niner mile final for
one-six right is a Boeing seven-fifty-seven, caution wake turbulence, wind two-six-zero at one-
three gust one-eight, runway one-six left cleared to land.” The traffic was Federal Express flight
1359 (FDX1359). The pilot acknowledged the clearance and informed the local controller they did
not have the B757 in sight. Also landing runway 16R preceding FDX1359 was United Parcel
Service flight 9706 (UPS9706), also a B757. (See attachment 2, figure 1.)

       At 1758:17, the pilot of N958CA reported seeing the traffic saying “five Charlie Alpha has
got a visual on the airliner for the, for the right.” There were no further recorded transmissions
from the pilot of N958CA. When the pilot of N958CA reported the “airliner” in sight, FDX1359
was about 5.95 nautical miles (NM) from the runway 16R threshold and UPS9706 was about 0.67
NM from the runway 16R threshold. (See attachment 2, figure 2.)

        At 1758:26, the RNO local controller informed the pilot of FDX1359 that there was traffic
“on a four-mile left base for one-six left six thousand two hundred feet, Bonanza, they do have
you in sight.” The pilot of FDX1359 acknowledged the traffic call and said they were also looking
for the traffic.

        At 1801:09, the RNO local controller instructed the pilot of Horizon Air flight 320
(QXE320) to line up and wait on runway 16R. The pilot acknowledged the instruction and asked
the RNO local controller “you see the flames off the end of ah the beginning of one-six right? Just,
there is some smoke going up now.” (See attachment 2, figure 3.)

2.0    Radar Data
        Radar data for this report was obtained from the FAA NCT terminal radar approach control
(TRACON). The NCT TRACON plot playback (.PPB) data was of good quality and was part of
the standard terminal automation replacement system (STARS). The FAA radar sensor providing
the radar data was an airport surveillance radar 11 (ASR-11) located on the RNO airport at latitude
39°30'4.55"N, and longitude 119°46'46.45"W. The RNO ATCT air traffic controllers used a
Tower Display Workstation (TDW) to receive radar data and assist working traffic.
3.0    Weather Information
       The 1755 RNO automated weather observation indicated winds from 290 degrees at 17
knots, gusting to 20 knots, visibility 10 miles, few clouds at 10,000 feet above ground level,
temperature 31 degrees Celsius (C), dew point minus 5 degrees C, and an altimeter setting of 30.01
inches of mercury.

       For further information, please see the Weather Group Chairman’s factual report.
4.0    FAA Airport Information
        RNO runway 16L was 9,000 feet long by 150 feet wide and was constructed of grooved
concrete. The runway approach end was located at 39-30.830430N and 119-46.004433W at an
elevation of 4414.8 feet msl. The threshold crossing height was 75 feet. The runway was served
by a 3.00 degree glide slope, and a 4-light Precision Approach Path Indicator (PAPI) light system,
installed on the left side of the runway at the approach end.

        RNO runway 16R was 11,001 feet long by 150 feet wide and was constructed of grooved
concrete. The runway approach end was located at 39-30.830635N and 119-46.153228W at an
elevation of 4414.8 feet msl. The runway had a displaced threshold of 999.3 feet at the approach
end of the runway. The threshold crossing height was 77 feet. The runway was served by a 3.06
degree glide slope, and a 4-light PAPI light system, installed on the left side of the runway at the
approach end.

        RNO runways 16R and 16L were parallel runways separated by 697.8 feet. (See attachment
2, figure 4.)

              NOTE− Consider parallel runways less than 2,500 feet apart as a single runway

                      1. Small behind large− 4 miles.

       7-2-1. VISUAL SEPARATION
       Aircraft may be separated by visual means, as provided in this paragraph, when other
